A whole book Series: Ezra, rebuilt · Article No. 3

The sound of two things at once

The old men weep. The young men shout. And no one standing there can tell the two sounds apart.

Ezra 3 · 10–13 The Margin 9 min read Read aloud available Also on Substack

Seventy years is long enough to forget the shape of a room. The people who had actually seen Solomon's temple — seen it standing, seen it burning — were old now, or gone. A whole generation had been born in Babylon and had never laid eyes on the thing they'd been weeping for by the rivers. And then, improbably, they were home, standing on the old ground, watching the first stones of a second temple go down into the dust.

You would expect one sound at a moment like that. Ezra records two.

Ezra 3 · 11–13

11…And all the people shouted with a great shout, when they praised the Lord, because the foundation of the house of the Lord was laid. 12But many of the priests and Levites and heads of fathers' houses, old men who had seen the first house, wept with a loud voice when they saw the foundation of this house being laid, though many shouted aloud for joy, 13so that the people could not distinguish the sound of the joyful shout from the sound of the people's weeping…

Read that last line slowly, because the text is doing something careful. It doesn't say some people were happy and some were sad, as if we could walk the crowd and take a census. It says the sound itself could not be told apart. The joy and the grief had risen into one noise, and from a distance — which is where the writer is standing — there was no separating them.

“The people could not distinguish the sound of joy from the sound of weeping.”

We are not good at this. We like our feelings sorted. We ask people how a funeral went, or a wedding, or a homecoming, and we expect a single word back — lovely, or hard. Scripture keeps refusing to sort them for us. It keeps showing us the moment where the two sounds are the same sound, and it treats that not as confusion but as truth.

What the old men were weeping for

It's tempting to read the old men as sourpuses — the ones who can't enjoy anything, who ruin the party by measuring the new against a glory they remember and the young have never seen. Later, the prophet Haggai seems to name exactly this: Who is left among you who saw this house in its former glory? How do you see it now? Is it not as nothing in your eyes? The second temple was smaller. Poorer. It would never hold what the first one held.

But grief is not the enemy of gratitude here, and the text won't let us pretend it is. The old men are weeping because they are home. You only weep like that over a thing you loved. Their tears are not a refusal of the joy; they are the price of having remembered. The young men can shout cleanly because they lost nothing — they never saw the first house. The old men carry a memory the young don't have, and memory is heavy, and it comes out as water.

✶ ✶ ✶

Here is the thing I keep coming back to. God does not send someone into that crowd to separate the sounds. No one stands up to say, the weeping is unbelief, please stop; only the shouting honors Me. The mingled noise is the worship. Both halves of it went up together, and both were received.

I think a lot of us are waiting to worship, or to give thanks, or to come home to God, until we can do it with a clean single feeling. Until the grief is finally gone and only the joy is left. And I think this small, strange verse in a book almost nobody reads is telling us not to wait that long. You are allowed to show up to the foundation-laying with both sounds in your chest. The God who hears it is not confused by you. He is not asking you to pick one.

“The mingled noise is the worship.”

Ezra is a book about starting over with less than you had. A smaller temple, a smaller people, a permission slip from a foreign king instead of a golden age. It is, quietly, a book for anyone rebuilding something that will never be what it was — a marriage, a faith, a body, a family, a city. And its most honest verse is the one where it admits that the rebuilding sounds like crying and cheering at the same time, and refuses to apologize for either.

Lay the foundation anyway. Make the sound you actually have.
